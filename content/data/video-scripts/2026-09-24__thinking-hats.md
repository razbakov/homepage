## Point 1 — The trap of logic (0:20–1:10)

Edward de Bono coined a term — lateral thinking. It means: stop following the obvious path. Most of us, when we face a problem, we do what we were taught. We analyze. We list options. We pick the best one. And that works — until it doesn't. Until someone rigs the game. De Bono said there are four things that unlock lateral thinking. First, notice the dominant idea that's trapping you. Second, look for different angles. Third, loosen the grip of rigid step-by-step logic. And fourth — welcome the unlikely idea. The one that feels too strange to say out loud. Because that's usually where the answer lives. Singapore needed a stadium for their National Day. Everyone assumed they'd build one. Instead, someone looked at the bay and said — what if the stage floats? That's not genius. That's just refusing to think inside the frame everyone handed you.

## Point 2 — The rigged game (1:10–2:10)

Now here's a story that makes this real. A farmer owes money to a moneylender. The moneylender wants to marry the farmer's daughter. So he proposes a deal — put a black pebble and a white pebble in a bag. She picks one. White, she's free and the debt is forgiven. Black, she marries him. Sounds fair. Except the girl watches him pick up two black pebbles. Both are black. The game is rigged. So what does she do? Think about it. Logic gives you three options. Refuse to pick — her father goes to jail. Call him a cheat — he's powerful, she's not, it ends badly. Or pick a black pebble and sacrifice herself. Every logical path leads to losing. That's the whole point. When the system is rigged, playing by the system's rules guarantees you lose.

`[B-ROLL: A hand reaching into a small bag, hesitating — fingers hovering over stones]`
`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "When the game is rigged, logic loses."]`

## Point 3 — The sideways move (2:10–3:05)

So what does she actually do? She reaches into the bag, pulls out a pebble — and fumbles it. Drops it onto the path, where it disappears among hundreds of other pebbles. Then she says: oh, how clumsy of me. But it's fine — just look at the one left in the bag. It's black. So mine must have been white. Game over. Debt forgiven. No marriage. She didn't fight the system. She didn't expose him. She changed the question. Instead of "which pebble did I pick?" she made it "which pebble is left?" That's lateral thinking. That's what de Bono built his entire framework around — including the Six Thinking Hats, which I break down in the full essay. The hats give you six different lenses to rotate through any problem. But the deeper lesson is this: the best answer is often the one that reframes what you're even being asked.
