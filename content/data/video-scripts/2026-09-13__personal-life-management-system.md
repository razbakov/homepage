# Daily — Why I Put My Life in a Git Repo

**Source:** https://razbakov.com/blog/2025-01-07-personal-life-management-system

## Hook (0:00–0:20)

I didn't know if my life was getting better. I had goals, projects, habits — all the stuff you're supposed to have. But no way to actually tell if any of it was working. So I did the most engineer thing possible. I put my life in a git repo.

## Point 1 — The honest mirror (0:20–1:10)

Here's the core of it. Every quarter, I score ten areas of my life from one to ten. Health. Relationships. Finances. Career. Spirituality. All of it. And I save that file with a date — and I never edit it after.

That's the rule. No going back and softening the threes. No quietly bumping a five to a seven because you feel better today. Git timestamps everything. Git shows you the diff. Health went from four to seven — great. Relationships dropped from eight to five — that's real, and now you have to look at it.

The first time I did this honestly, it hurt. But you can't fix what you won't measure.

## Point 2 — Reviews over goals (1:10–2:10)

Now, everyone loves setting goals. That's the easy part. You write down "get healthier" in January, feel great about it, and forget by March. I did that for years.

What actually changed things was the review loop. Daily check-in — two minutes. What did I do, what am I doing tomorrow. Weekly review — thirty minutes on Saturday. Did I do what I said I'd do? Where are the gaps? And then the quarterly assessment — rescore everything, compare, set new objectives.

The trick is — I use AI as a thinking partner. Not for advice. For memory. It reads my whole repo — my OKRs, my reviews, my decisions — and it catches the stuff I'd miss. Like: "You said you'd focus on one project, but you have five active. Which four are you pausing?" It doesn't judge. It just remembers. And that turns out to be more useful than wisdom.

## Point 3 — Why plain text wins (2:10–3:05)

I've tried every app. Notion, Obsidian, habit trackers, journaling apps. They all have the same problem — they own the format. When the app dies, your data goes with it.

Markdown in git is forever. I can read these files in any text editor, on any machine, twenty years from now. I can search them, diff them, pipe them into scripts. And there's something about the plainness that keeps me honest. No dashboards. No gamification. No dopamine for checking a box. Just words asking me to tell the truth about where I am.

And here's the thing I didn't expect — writing "I didn't exercise because I was tired" into a file that gets committed to git history… you don't want to type that same sentence again next week. The system changes your behavior just by making it visible.
