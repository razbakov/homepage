# Daily — Why OpenClaw Has No Soul (And How I Built My Own)

**Source:** https://razbakov.com/blog/2026-03-25-why-openclaw-has-no-soul

## Hook (0:00–0:20)

OpenClaw has no soul. I know that sounds harsh — everyone's calling it revolutionary. An AI with identity, with personality. But I looked at it and saw a Telegram bot with a system prompt. And I know, because I built something real a full year before they launched.

## Point 1 — The branding trick (0:20–1:10)

Here's what OpenClaw actually gives you. One AI character. Connected to Telegram. You chat with it. It responds. That's the product. The "soul" they market? It's a system prompt. The "identity"? Whatever you type into a text field. There's no memory architecture. No goal orientation. No multi-perspective thinking. It's Claude in a wrapper with great branding. And look — great branding works. People are excited. But excitement isn't architecture. A chatbot that sounds cool is still a chatbot.

`[B-ROLL: Side-by-side — OpenClaw's character setup screen vs. a raw system prompt in a code editor]`
`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "A system prompt is not a soul."]`

## Point 2 — Six agents, not one (1:10–2:10)

In January 2025 — a year before OpenClaw — I built what I called an AI secretary. One agent. But it quickly became six. Maya plans my day. Viktor handles technical decisions. Luna writes content. Marco thinks strategy. Sage coaches me. Kai manages relationships. Each has a distinct personality. When I say "hi" in the team chat, they all react differently. Marco obsesses about my festival. Luna asks what's for lunch. Viktor says nothing unless there's a technical question. But here's the real test. Send the same message to two of them. Tell Maya, "I'm skipping dinner tonight." She says, "Got it, I'll update the calendar." Tell Sage the same thing. She says, "Hold on. You've been skipping meals when you're stressed. What's actually going on?" That's the difference between a bot and a team. Multiple perspectives on the same situation.

`[B-ROLL: Telegram chat showing different agent responses to the same message, split screen]`
`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "A bot executes. A team questions."]`

## Point 3 — Why friction is the feature (2:10–3:05)

Generic AI assistants are yes-machines. They do what you say. That's useful for tasks. It's terrible for decisions. When you have six agents with different priorities, you get disagreement. You get challenge. You get the kind of friction that makes better decisions — the same thing a real executive team provides. And I learned something building this. Personality requires constraints. Agents without specific domains drift into generic helpfulness. Goals beat instructions. Once I gave them their own OKRs, they became proactive. And the team metaphor — thinking of agents as people, not tools — that changes everything about how you interact with them.
