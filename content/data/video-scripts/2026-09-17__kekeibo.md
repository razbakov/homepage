## Point 1 — No app could fix what was broken (0:20–1:10)

I spent years chasing the perfect budgeting app. Toshl, Pennies, dozens of others. They all wanted the same thing from me — discipline I didn't have. Track every coffee. Log every receipt. Every single day.

So I went simpler. Google Sheets for monthly planning. A Telegram bot to log expenses on the go. Even a little script that pulled transactions from my bank automatically. Clever setup, right? It worked — until my laziness caught up. I'd skip a week. Then a month. Then the whole system felt like homework I'd already failed.

Here's what I finally understood: if a tool punishes you for taking a break, it's the wrong tool. The system has to survive your worst month, not just your best one.

## Point 2 — Kakeibo: budget with envelopes, not spreadsheets (1:10–2:10)

Then I found Kakeibo. It's a Japanese budgeting method — been around over a hundred years. The core idea is dead simple. You take your income, subtract your fixed costs and savings, and what's left goes into four envelopes. Needs — groceries, medicine. Wants — going out, shopping. Culture — books, courses. And Extra — gifts, surprises.

That's it. You don't track every transaction. You don't categorize your morning coffee. You just know: here's what I have, split four ways. The question becomes not "where did my money go" but "how much can I spend right now without hurting myself later?"

I stopped tracking the noise. Regular bills, subscriptions — the bank already knows those. I only needed to watch what I actually control. That shift — from accounting to awareness — that's the whole game.

## Point 3 — Ikigai built it, Kaizen shipped it (2:10–3:05)

But paper envelopes don't work when everything's digital. I needed an app that could answer one question: how much can I safely spend today? Nothing on the market did it simply enough. So I built it myself. Programming is my ikigai — the thing I'm good at, that I love, that the world needs.

I sketched on paper first. Got a prototype running in a day. Then hit a wall on day two — the code felt heavy, the path unclear. That's when Kaizen showed up. One small question: what is the smallest next step? Install one library. Define one data structure. Wire up one screen. Each tiny step made the next one obvious.

By the end of that day, I had a working app. You set a budget, it divides by the days left, subtracts what you've spent, and shows one number. Overspend on Monday? It recalculates for the rest of the week. Take a month off? Just log your balance and pick up where you left off. No guilt. No backlog.
