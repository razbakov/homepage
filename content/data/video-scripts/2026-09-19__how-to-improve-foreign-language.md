# Daily — How to Improve Foreign Language Skills

**Source:** https://razbakov.com/blog/2018-08-18-how-to-improve-foreign-language

## Hook (0:00–0:20)

Most people fail at languages before they even start. They download Duolingo, do it for nine days, and quit. And here's why — they skipped the only step that actually matters. They never decided *why* they wanted it in the first place.

## Point 1 — Dream Before You Plan (0:20–1:10)

Before you touch a single flashcard, before you sign up for anything — sit down and ask yourself what fluency actually looks like for you. Not the vague "I want to speak Spanish" thing. I mean — picture the conversation. Who are you talking to? Where are you? What are you saying? Write it down. Draw it if you have to. I use something called YearCompass to set a year-long intention, but the tool doesn't matter. What matters is that the goal becomes vivid enough that your brain treats it as real. And then — here's the counterintuitive part — you let it go. You don't force it. You let the desire settle until you feel genuinely ready to begin. Because a goal you pressure yourself into is a goal you'll abandon by February.

`[B-ROLL: Journal open on a table, hand sketching a scene — a café, two people talking]`
`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "Make the goal vivid. Then let it settle."]`

## Point 2 — Structure Without Suffocation (1:10–2:10)

Once you're ready, you need a plan — but not the kind that makes you miserable. I'm talking SMART goals. Specific. Measurable. And broken down into actual activities you can track. Two long-term goals, two mid-term, two short-term. That's it. The key is doing *something* every single day without interruption. Five minutes counts. Five minutes absolutely counts. But here's what most people miss — you have to rotate. Don't just do flashcards every day until your eyes glaze over. One day you sing along to a song in that language. Next day you listen to a podcast. Then you read an article. Then you watch a movie with subtitles. The variety is what keeps your brain engaged. And every time you encounter a foreign text, run it through the full cycle — read it, hear it, say it, translate it, research the history behind the expression, add it to your vocabulary, and then actually use it in a real conversation. That loop is everything.

`[B-ROLL: Split screen — someone singing along to lyrics on a laptop / someone watching a foreign film with subtitles]`
`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "5 minutes a day. Rotate the activity."]`

## Point 3 — Make It Social (2:10–3:05)

Now here's where it gets personal. The fastest way I've improved any language is by making it a relationship, not a curriculum. Find friends who are also learning — correct each other, debate the hard parts. Find native speakers and do a tandem — you teach them your language, they teach you theirs. Follow people on social media who speak that language and actually comment on their posts. Start broadcasting yourself — make videos, write posts *in* that language about something you already know well. And if you really want to accelerate, do public speaking. Join a Toastmasters group in your target language. Give a talk at a meetup. That's terrifying, I know. But nothing forces fluency like standing in front of people with no safety net. Language isn't vocabulary and grammar rules. It's culture. It's people. You learn it by living inside it.
