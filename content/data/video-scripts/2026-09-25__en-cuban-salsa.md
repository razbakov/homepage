## Hook (0:00–0:20)

Salsa isn't what you think it is. Most people hear "salsa" and picture a nightclub in New York, maybe a couple spinning in a straight line. But the real thing — the source — comes from Havana. And it moves in circles, not lines. That difference matters more than you'd expect.

## Point 1 — The Music Is Alive (0:20–1:10)

Let's start with the sound. In Cuba, the salsa sound is called timba. And timba is not polite. It's fast, it's layered, it pulls from rumba, from reggae, from hip hop — it blends everything the island has absorbed. A timba song doesn't just play one rhythm. It starts slow, breaks into a core groove, then opens up into this call-and-response where the lead singer improvises over a repeating vocal pattern. Ahi Na Ma.

And when the rhythm shifts — when the band drops into a reggaeton break or a hip hop groove — the dancers respond. They pull apart and start doing these incredible body isolations, shudders, movements that come straight from Afro-Cuban rumba. The music and the dance are in constant conversation. One doesn't exist without the other. Bands like Los Van Van or NG La Banda — they built this sound. And it's still evolving.

## Point 2 — Dancing in Circles (1:10–2:10)

Now here's the thing about the dance itself. Cubans don't call it salsa. They call it casino. Named after the old Havana casinos — not the gambling, the dance halls. That's where the big bands played, that's where people went to move. When the revolution shut the casinos down, people kept dancing casino in community halls. The name stuck.

And casino moves differently than what you see in LA or New York. It's circular. The lead is constantly moving around the follower — checking her out, showing her off. Exhibala. There's no slot, no fixed line. It's fluid, it's playful, it's three-dimensional. And then you add rueda — the wheel. A whole circle of couples dancing synchronized moves, exchanging partners every few beats, one person calling out the names. Dile que no. Enchufla. Adios. Sometimes a hundred couples in one circle. It started in the 1950s at the Casino Deportivo in Havana — dancers competing, inventing new moves all week, showing them off on the weekend.

## Point 3 — Culture You Can't Export Clean (2:10–3:05)

What I love about this is how local it stays, even as it spreads. In Chile, there's a rueda move called Entel Chile — you mime talking on a phone, after the country's biggest phone company. In Miami, there's one called Balsero — it imitates ocean waves, because balsero means someone who came to the US by raft. You won't find that move in Chile. Nobody arrives in Chile by raft. The dance absorbs the place it's in. It's not a frozen choreography — it's a living language. Every city that picks it up adds its own vocabulary. And some moves are just funny. There's one called Fly where everyone claps over each other's heads pretending to catch a fly ball. Because what's the point of dancing if you're not laughing?
