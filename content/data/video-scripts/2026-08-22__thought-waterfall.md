## Point 1 — The Cascade (0:20–1:10)
About ten years ago I started doing brain dumps. Set a timer, write without stopping, no editing. And something weird happened. I'd write one idea, read it back, and that sentence would trigger another idea. That one triggered a third. Within minutes I wasn't writing — I was chasing a cascade of connections. Things I hadn't thought about in months were suddenly linking together.

There's a framework for this — Edward De Bono's lateral thinking. The brain is a self-organizing system that follows established patterns. Free writing disrupts those patterns. And sometimes, multiple disruptions cascade at once. The brain starts finding connections faster than your conscious mind can track.

That's the waterfall. And it is nothing like flow state. Flow is calm, single-threaded, steady. The waterfall is exciting — almost overwhelming. Multiple threads collide. One idea strengthens another, spawns a third, and they all connect in a burst. The risk in flow is losing time. In the waterfall, you lose *ideas*.

`[B-ROLL: Whiteboard filling up with connected notes, arrows drawn fast between clusters]`
`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "Flow is calm. The waterfall is a flood."]`

## Point 2 — The AI Accelerant (1:10–2:10)
Here's what brought the waterfall back stronger than ever: AI sub-agents.

I launch an AI agent with a prompt, and while it's running, my mind keeps processing. "How could this connect to that other thing?" The agent returns results, those results spark new connections, I launch another prompt. The cycle accelerates.

Here's why it works. Normally you have an idea and it goes into a queue. A to-do list, a backlog, a "someday" note. Most ideas die there. But when you can externalize an idea as a prompt and get something concrete back in minutes — the thought doesn't die. It transforms. And that frees your mind for the next one.

The AI becomes a thinking partner that keeps pace with the cascade. Each result is fuel for the next wave. And I think as this becomes more common — as the gap between "what if" and "let's see" shrinks to minutes — more people are going to experience this. Brains given permission to generate freely don't just generate. They cascade.

## Point 3 — The Bucket (2:10–3:05)
But here's the honest part. During the waterfall, everything feels brilliant. Every connection seems profound. Then you look at your notes the next morning and — some of it is genuinely good. Some of it is nonsense. And some of it is an interesting seed buried in excited rambling.

So the waterfall needs two systems. During — frictionless capture. Voice notes, bullet points, not full sentences. If you stop to write proper paragraphs, you lose three ideas saving one. After — process with a clear head. Which ideas survive scrutiny? Which ones were just the excitement talking? The good ones get routed into projects. The rest go to archive.

You can't force the waterfall, but you can create conditions. Morning free writing. Working across multiple projects. Anything that removes friction and keeps the brain's editor from intercepting. The moment you start judging, the waterfall stops.
