# Daily — Severance, but for AI

**Source:** https://razbakov.com/blog/2026-04-07-severance-but-for-ai

## Hook (0:00–0:20)

What if your AI shouldn't know everything about you? I've been watching *Severance*, and buried inside the horror of splitting a person in two — there's actually a design pattern. One I think changes how we should build AI entirely. The boundary is the product.

## Point 1 — Avatars, not assistants (0:20–1:10)

Here's what I keep circling back to. Everyone's racing to build the one assistant that knows everything. Your calendar, your therapy notes, your Slack drama, your midnight doubts — all in one context window. And I think that's wrong.

What I actually need is not one assistant. It's two versions of me. A personal avatar that knows my goals, my relationships, what kind of life I'm building. And a work avatar that knows my projects, my deadlines, my role. I don't want those two sharing memory by default. I don't want my work assistant carrying my private reflections into a standup. And I don't want Slack-brain leaking into the rest of my life.

That's not just privacy. That's clarity. Different parts of you need different contexts to think well.

`[B-ROLL: Split screen — left side: journal, coffee, morning light; right side: code editor, Slack notifications, meeting grid]`
`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "Different parts of you need different contexts to think well."]`

## Point 2 — Characters, not features (1:10–2:10)

Now — once you have the boundary, you can put agents around it. And this is where it gets interesting. I want Sage when I need reflection. Maya when I need someone to turn chaos into motion. Viktor when I need technical depth. Names matter. Faces matter. Humans remember characters better than features.

And the beautiful part? The same character can exist on both sides of the boundary. There can be a personal Maya and a work Maya. Same archetype. Different memory. Different priorities. Different sense of what "help" even means. That feels so much more human than reinventing a fictional cast every time a new project shows up.

You stack it in layers. Avatar for the self. Organization for the shared mission. Project for the execution context. Agents as the personalities that help you navigate each one. And suddenly it's not abstract philosophy — it's something you can actually build.

`[B-ROLL: Diagram animation — concentric circles expanding outward: avatar → agents → organization → projects]`
`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "Humans remember characters better than features."]`

## Point 3 — The collision is the point (2:10–3:05)

But here's the part I can't stop thinking about. The most interesting thing is not the separation. It's what happens when you let the two sides talk on purpose.

Your work self wants the promotion. Your personal self thinks it'll cost too much. Most AI today would smooth that tension out immediately. Give you a nice balanced answer. But maybe tension is exactly what you need to see. Maybe the point is not to collapse the conflict — it's to stage it clearly enough that you can finally decide.

Should I take the new role? Should I move cities? Should I start the company? These questions are never purely professional or purely personal. They live in the collision. And if AI is going to be a real collaborator, it needs to hold that collision — not erase it.
