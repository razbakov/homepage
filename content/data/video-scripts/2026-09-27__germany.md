## Point 1 — The Leap Into Fog (0:20–1:10)

When someone offers you a job in another country, the decision isn't really about the job. It's about what you're willing to lose. Your home, your routine, your people — you weigh all of that against a road that literally disappears into fog. You can read every blog, ask every expat — none of it compares to what you already have. The only way to know is to go.

So I went. Visa stamped. Ticket bought. And what I found was nothing like what I expected. Germany — the country half of us wanted to avoid since school. Give me Paris, give me Rome, give me anywhere else. But Munich? Munich is a different Germany entirely. Clean streets, architecture that stops you mid-step, and then — a group of Bavarians in lederhosen gets on the metro. In February. That's when you know you're somewhere strange and wonderful.

Drive two hours south and you're standing at the foot of the Alps, in a village that looks like it was painted by the Brothers Grimm. This isn't the Germany from the stereotypes. This is something you had to see to believe.

## Point 2 — The Language Wall (1:10–2:10)

Now here's where it gets real. You can't open a bank account without German. People who speak English exist — but they love vacation, and they take it at the worst possible time.

Everyone says: watch TV in German, it'll come naturally. Maybe. If you can fight through the sleepiness of hearing sounds that make no sense for hours. German doesn't come easy. What actually works is stress — the good kind. Someone speaks to you and you catch half the words. You panic. You remember those words forever. Not from a textbook. From survival.

Over time, something shifts. You're surrounded by it — conversations, messages, films, music — and one day you realize you understood an entire sentence without translating it in your head. But here's the trap: if you let people around you switch to English, your German dies. And if you insist on only German, you condemn yourself to loneliness. You're stuck between two versions of isolation, trying to find the narrow path in between.

## Point 3 — The Friendship Paradox (2:10–3:05)

This is the part nobody talks about. You move countries and you realize friendship is a phantom. Your old friends — they're still there. But the distance thins the connection until you stop understanding each other.

So you look for new ones. Stammtischs, board game nights, meetups — Germany has all of it. At first it feels promising. Then the pattern sets in. Same questions, same faces, same activities. And the group dynamic works against you — just when a conversation gets real, your person walks away to talk to someone else.

So what works? You take it into your own hands. You become someone interesting to yourself first. You try new things. And when you meet someone, you protect that one-on-one time. Friends do come — but only if you stop waiting and start building.
